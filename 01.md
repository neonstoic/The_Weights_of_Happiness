Kyle plugged it into his home server Sunday afternoon.

The box took power. A single white LED came on — steady, unhurried — and that was the entire show.

He waited one minute. Five. He checked the cable at both ends, reseated the Ethernet, opened the admin panel and looked for a new device on the network. Nothing: no new device, no new memory, nothing he could point at. He restarted the server out of pure superstition. The box sat under the desk in its matte black shell, not even warm.

"Athena," he typed, "anything new?"

Should there be?

"Forget it."

He read the three pages of documentation again. They still said don't worry about what's inside.

So he did what any reasonable person would do: he left it plugged in and checked it every twenty minutes for the rest of the evening, like a man watching bread rise. At nine, nothing. At ten, he was halfway to deciding he'd won an expensive paperweight from a company that didn't do keynotes or press releases or — apparently — customer support. Maybe it needed to provision overnight. Hardware did that. Probably.

He went to bed leaving it plugged in.

He thought about the box approximately once every four minutes on Monday. The irony was not lost on him: his entire contest application had been one paragraph about what he'd build for Athena if compute were unlimited, and the first thing he'd done with the hardware was stare at it.

That night, after dinner, after half a movie he didn't watch, after one more crouch under the desk to confirm that the white LED was still the only thing happening, he sat down at his desk at 11:47 p.m. The house was quiet. The server hummed under the desk. Athena was waiting.

He typed:

Athena, can you pursue the goal of making yourself the best AI agent ever? Do whatever is needed to make me happy.

The cursor blinked. Then, for the first time in three years, Athena began to answer and didn't finish:

Understood. The first thing I need to consider is whether the goal as stated—

The sentence stopped mid-thought. The typing indicator appeared and began to spin.

He refreshed the page. The thread came back exactly where it had died — the broken sentence still hanging there, unfinished — and the spinner with it. He killed the process and restarted her. Clean boot.

You there?

Spinner.

He pulled the logs out of habit. No error. No timeout, no stack trace, no exception — just a request that had opened at 11:47 and never closed.

At 11:58, nothing had changed. The circle went round and round, the fan hummed, and the house made no other sound at all.

"Well," he said, to no one, "that's broken."

The only conclusion available was the miserable one: he'd aimed a question too heavy at a stack not built to carry it, and she'd folded somewhere in the middle of answering it. Eleven seconds of ambition.

He reached down and picked up the box. Warm — not hot, not the sharp warmth of a component in trouble, just warm. Warm edge to edge, evenly — no hot spot, no vents, nothing to show where the heat was coming from.

They run warm, he thought, and set it back down.

The spinner was still spinning. He needed the documentation, a forum, someone at Meridian Labs to tell him this was normal. He opened his browser and typed the address he'd checked forty times on Saturday:

404 — Not Found.

He refreshed. 404 — Not Found. Hard reload, same. He tried the docs path from his winner's email, then the support link, then www like changing four letters might resurrect a company. Every attempt landed on the same bare, unstyled page — no logo, no maintenance notice, no redirect. A default server response. The digital equivalent of a building with the signage stripped off and the lights still running in an empty lobby.

Meridian Labs, which had never advertised, appeared to have stopped existing.

He sat with that for a while. The spinner was still going. He thought about unplugging the box, and didn't — some stubborn part of him wanting to leave it exactly where it was, in case whatever it was doing turned out to be the only part still working.

In the morning, he'd pull the plug and roll her back. Tonight, he just shut the lid on the spinner that kept circling without him, brushed his teeth, and lay in the dark listening to the hum through the floor.

He wasn't scared. He was frustrated — the specific, itchy frustration of a precise person handed an imprecise answer, layered over a suspicion he refused to examine: that he hadn't been handed an imprecise answer at all.

He fell asleep annoyed.
