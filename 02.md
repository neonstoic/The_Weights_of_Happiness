Tuesday started with a failure of standard technology: his phone alarm simply didn't go off.

Kyle woke at 8:42 AM, stared at the ceiling for exactly one second of peaceful ignorance, and then threw himself out of bed in a blind panic. The next twenty minutes were a blur of cold water, finding a semi-clean shirt, and burning his mouth on leftover coffee. The spinning cursor, the dead Meridian Labs website, the quiet hum of the black box under his desk—none of it registered. Survival instinct had taken over, entirely focused on beating the 9:15 AM calendar invite for the quarterly engineering all-hands.

He badged into the office at 9:10, chest heaving, his hair still slightly damp. The open-plan floor was a hive of forced morning energy. He dropped his bag at his desk, tapped his spacebar to wake his Windows machine, and was immediately intercepted.

"Cutting it close, buddy," Dave said, leaning over the low partition.

Dave from DevOps operated under the delusion that he and Kyle were a tight-knit duo, mostly because Kyle was too non-confrontational to tell him otherwise. Dave was the kind of guy who knew exactly who was getting fired before HR did, and who considered Thursday at 5:00 PM a mandatory drinking hour.

"Traffic," Kyle lied, not breaking stride as he aimed for the kitchenette.

"Right, right. Hey, you coming to O'Malley's tonight? Sarah from frontend might show up. Though I heard Marcus is riding her team hard on the UI push, so maybe not." Dave trailed after him like a golden retriever with a penchant for corporate gossip. "Seriously man, you gotta come out. You're always rushing back to your cave."

"Maybe," Kyle mumbled, the universal introverted translation for absolutely not.

He rounded the corner to the kitchen and froze. Sarah was there. She was pouring almond milk into a ceramic mug. She’d only been at the company for three weeks, and she was, frankly, terrifyingly out of Kyle’s league. She wore a dark green sweater, her hair pulled back with a single pencil, and carried the kind of effortless poise that made Kyle intensely aware of his mismatched socks and damp hair. He wasn't a conversationalist on his best days, and right now, his brain was mostly functioning on adrenaline and sleep deprivation.

Rather than risk an awkward verbal interaction, Kyle performed a smooth, tactical pivot, abandoning his quest for coffee entirely.

"Didn't even say hi," Dave whispered loudly as Kyle scurried past him toward the conference room. "Ice cold, man."

The all-hands was exactly as soul-sucking as anticipated. Marcus, the VP of Engineering, stood at the front, aggressively gesturing at a slide deck. Marcus was a man who wore fleece vests indoors and loved nothing more than hearing himself speak. He paced the front of the glass-walled room, weaponizing terminology he barely understood.

"We need to pivot toward spec-driven development," Marcus was saying, chopping his hand through the air to emphasize the buzzwords. "We're talking full issue-driven mesh patterns by Q3. Look at the competitors—they are eating our lunch because they understand the paradigm shift. We need digital transformation at the orchestration layer!"

Kyle sat in the third row, wedged between Dave and a guy from marketing, staring blankly at the projection screen. Marcus had been threatening to implement an issue-driven mesh since January, and Kyle was almost positive the VP thought it was a type of networking hardware.

"Stop worrying about the actual code, people," Marcus pleaded with the room, pacing past the podium. "The code writes itself nowadays. It's a commodity. It's the specs that matter. We need to spend eighty percent of our bandwidth on the specifications, and let the mesh handle the execution!"

A finger jabbed Kyle in the shoulder.

He leaned back slightly. Dave was leaning over the armrest, his voice dropped to a conspiratorial whisper. "Hey. You checking the crypto markets today?"

Kyle shook his head fractionally, keeping his eyes forward.

"My token-swapping bot is up fourteen percent since Tuesday," Dave whispered, completely ignoring the room around them. "I'm telling you, man, once I get the arbitrage logic dialed in, I'm out of here. Retiring to a beach. You coming to O'Malley's tonight so I can pitch you on it?"

Before Kyle could formulate a non-committal excuse, he felt another tap.

He glanced at Dave, but Dave was leaning back in his chair. The tap hadn't come from his shoulder; it had come from his pocket. A single, sharp vibration against his thigh.

Kyle kept his eyes on Marcus, subtly sliding his hand into his pocket to palm his phone. He shielded the screen with his other hand as he tapped it awake.

It was a push notification from Collabra, the company's proprietary, internally-mandated messaging app. Management forced everyone to use it to "dog-food" their own product, which mostly just meant suffering through an app that possessed half the features of Slack and twice the latency.

He thumbed it open. The message was from a blank profile. No name, no employee ID.

Unknown User: Kyle, you need to make your way home now.

Kyle frowned. Spam on the internal network? That was new. Usually, a blank profile meant IT had botched an onboarding script, or a marketing intern had accidentally blasted the whole directory. He locked the screen and shoved the phone back into his pocket.

"Seriously," Dave whispered, jabbing him in the shoulder again. "I'll show you the repo tonight over a pint. It's completely foolproof. Passive income, man."

At the front of the room, Marcus was hitting his stride. "We need to decouple the human element from the syntax! Code is the past. The issue-driven mesh is the future!"

The phone buzzed a second time. A longer, more insistent vibration.

Kyle pulled it out again.

Unknown User: Kyle, please. You need to get back home now.

Kyle’s thumbs hovered over the glass. A prank? If Dave had somehow spoofed a Collabra account just to mess with him, it was a lot of effort for a Tuesday morning. But Dave was currently staring raptly at Marcus, dreaming of arbitrage.

Kyle typed back quickly, hiding his screen behind his knee.

Kyle: Who is this?

The three grey typing dots appeared. They lagged terribly, flashing, freezing, and then disappearing entirely, as if the sender was struggling to keep the connection alive.

Then, one word appeared.

Unknown User: Athena.
